The 30-second answer

"We learned a model of how our robot actually moves, then used it to choose every command."

A small neural network was trained on 230 logged real-robot runs to predict, from the ball's velocity and the command, where the ball will be one step later and how fast it will be going. Each control step, the controller tries 30 candidate commands, rolls each one forward through that model for 1.2 s, and also checks where the ball would come to rest if the drive were cut. It sends the cheapest one: most progress along the A* route, least drift off the centreline, no predicted overshoot past the next corner or the goal, no predicted wall contact. A safety layer handles jams.

  1. Sense. The robot reports its locator position, heading and speed (about every 0.2 s over Bluetooth).
  2. Estimate. The Lab 2 EKF fuses that with the motion model into the state [x, y, θ, v] in maze coordinates.
  3. Plan. Lab 3's A* gives the route: start (0,4) → (0,2) → (1,2) → (1,0) → goal (4,0).
  4. Choose (the learned part). 30 candidate commands are rolled through the learned model in one batch, scored, and the cheapest is picked.
  5. Check. The safety layer passes it through, or takes over if the ball is jammed, stalled or off the corridor.
  6. Act. The command goes to the robot; repeat.
Q1

What learning method did you use?

Learned dynamics model + model-predictive control. This is the first option on Ed #140: "learn a dynamics model, and use your existing navigation stack to optimise with respect to this".

  • What is learned: the robot's one-step dynamics, i.e. where it goes and how fast, given its velocity and command. Supervised regression on logged real data.
  • How it is used: sampling-based MPC. A* still decides the route; every command is chosen by predicting the ball through the learned model.
  • Before this we tried behaviour cloning (copying the Lab 3 A* + PID expert). It is still in the code as a fallback; see Q2 for why it was replaced.
Q2

Why did you choose this method?

Every hardware failure in the first Lab 4 runs was a dynamics problem, not a planning problem:

  • The ball keeps rolling after the drive is cut: 5.5 cm on average, up to about 12 cm. It overshot corners and the goal.
  • A command of 0.042 produced about 0.13 m/s on the floor: commanded speed is not real speed.
  • There is actuation latency: right after a command from rest, the ball barely moves for a step.

Behaviour cloning

A memoryless copy of the expert. It never learned the coast, rounded every corner (7.2 cm error at the tightest), and on its own reached the goal in 0 of 10 simulator runs. It can only be as good as the expert it copies.

Reinforcement learning

Would train in the simulator, but the simulator barely coasts and has no gap between commanded and real speed, so the policy would not transfer. Training on the robot would need far more runs than we have.

A learned dynamics model learns exactly what was wrong (the coast, the speed gain, the latency) from data we already had, and the MPC uses it to brake in time for each corner instead of using hand-tuned constants.

The learned dynamics model (the part that is trained)

Everything is in the frame of the commanded heading: "forward" is along the direction the ball was told to go, "lateral" is perpendicular. That makes the model independent of where the ball is in the maze, and a turn shows up as lateral velocity.

Why the previous command: the first version had only velocity and the current command. Starting from rest it saw "at rest, commanded" and predicted no motion on every step, so the controller never left the start. Adding the previous command fixed that.

Q4

What does your loss function do?

Training loss of the dynamics model

L = (1 / (N·4)) · Σi=1..N Σk=1..4 ( (ŷik − yik) / σk )²
# dynamics_learning.py, train()
loss = (((model(xt[idx]) - yt[idx]) / ys) ** 2).mean()
  • ŷ: the model's prediction of the 4 outputs (displacement and next velocity); y: what the ball really did in the log.
  • σk (ys = y_std): the standard deviation of output k over the training set.
  • Mean over the batch and the 4 outputs: it is a mean squared error.

In words: it measures how far the predicted motion is from the real motion, with each output measured in units of its own spread. Displacements are millimetres and velocities are tenths of a metre per second, so without dividing by σ the loss would only care about the velocity terms. Normalising makes all four count equally.

Model selection: after every epoch the same loss is computed on held-out runs, and the weights from the epoch with the lowest held-out loss are kept (0.299 for the deployed model, i.e. about 70% of each output's variance explained, one step ahead).

The MPC's cost (what it minimises when choosing a command)

J = −30·progress + 600·Σ lateral² + 8000·overshoot² + 3000·overshoot + 10000·[wall] + 0.02·|Δheading|
TermWhat it rewards or penalisesWeight
progressdistance gained along the current route segment over the 1.2 s horizon−30
lateral²drifting off the centreline (squared, summed over the horizon)600
overshootpredicted resting point past the next corner (or 4 cm short of the goal), if the drive were cut now8000 (sq) + 3000 (lin)
[wall]any predicted point touching a physical wall10000
|Δheading|swinging the heading about0.02

The "drive cut" test is what replaced hand-tuned braking: the controller only goes as fast as the learned coast says it can still stop at the corner.

If asked about the earlier behaviour-cloning loss

L = MSE(speed̂ / max_speed, speed / max_speed) + λ · mean((sinθ̂ − sinθ)² + (cosθ̂ − cosθ)²), λ = 1

Heading as sin/cos so −π and +π are the same direction. Trained on 1,522 rows from passing Lab 3 runs; it was replaced because it could not learn the coast.

Q6

How did you train the model?

The TA focus: how the training function processes its inputs. Two functions do it.

1. load_run(): one logged run → training pairs (X, Y)

for t in range(1, len(a) - 1):                    # needs t-1 and t+1
    if dt outside 0.05..1.2 s: skip                 # stalls / batched samples
    step_in  = pos[t]   - pos[t-1]                  # how it arrived
    step_out = pos[t+1] - pos[t]                    # what it did next (the target)
    if either step > 0.15 m: skip                   # locator glitch
    heading = cmd[t].heading                        # commanded heading = the frame
    vf, vl   = to_cmd_frame(step_in / dt_in, heading)    # input velocity
    df, dl   = to_cmd_frame(step_out, heading)           # target displacement
    vf2, vl2 = to_cmd_frame(step_out / dt_out, heading)  # target velocity
    turn = wrap(cmd[t-1].heading - heading)
    pf, pl = cmd[t-1].speed * cos(turn), cmd[t-1].speed * sin(turn)
    X.append([vf, vl, cmd[t].speed, pf, pl, dt_out])
    Y.append([df, dl, vf2, vl2])

2. train(): fit the network

  1. Shuffle the runs (seed 0); hold out 20% of runs as validation.
  2. Compute x_mean, x_std, y_mean, y_std on the training transitions and store them inside the model.
  3. For 400 epochs: shuffle the transitions, step through them in batches of 512, compute the normalised MSE (Q4), back-propagate, Adam step.
  4. After each epoch, compute the same loss on the held-out runs; keep the best weights.
  5. Report errors on the held-out runs against two baselines and save dynamics_model_real.pth.
python labs/lab4/dynamics_learning.py --source real --epochs 400

Q8

What changes did you make?

In the order they were made, each one in response to something seen on the robot.

  1. Behaviour cloning first. Network copying the Lab 3 expert, with a safety layer; corners and the coast were the weak point.
  2. Learned dynamics + MPC. Learned the coast, speed gain and latency from all logged runs.
  3. Previous command as an input. Without it the model predicted no motion from rest and the ball never left the start.
  4. Overshoot priced at every corner, not only the goal. Corner 3 had been running 4–6 cm past.
  5. Aim 4 cm short of the goal. The ball consistently finished 4–6 cm past the centre.
  6. At rest, never choose to stay stopped. The MPC parked at corners because the model is weakest at starting from standstill.
  7. Safety layer (push-and-check). Detects a stall, a collision flag or slow progress, pushes along the route, then checks whether the ball really moved.
  8. Slow approaches into corners 2 and 3. Fewer wall hits at the tight turns.
  9. Honest speed scale. The old scale of 20 was silently clipped to motor power raw 45; now 1.93 means raw 45 and higher really is faster.

Likely follow-up questions

QuestionShort answer
Why split by run, not by time step?Neighbouring steps are nearly identical; mixing them across train and validation would make the validation score look better than it is.
Why normalise the loss?The outputs have different units and sizes (mm vs m/s); dividing by each output's spread makes all four count equally.
Why work in the commanded-heading frame?The physics is the same wherever the ball is in the maze; the frame removes position and orientation so the model only learns how the ball responds to a command.
Is it still "learning" if MPC chooses the command?Yes: the dynamics the MPC plans with are learned from data. This is the model-based option listed on Ed #140.
What happens when the model is wrong?The MPC re-plans every step from the latest estimate, so errors do not build up; jams and stalls are handled by the safety layer.
Why not reinforcement learning?The simulator does not reproduce the coast or the speed gain, so a simulator-trained policy would not transfer, and there is not enough robot time to train on hardware.
Known weakness?From standstill the model believes a bigger command moves the ball less (little start-up data). Handled in the controller: from rest it only chooses "stop" or a strong push.
What does the submitted M4-2 file contain?The same pipeline in one file: the EKF, the A* planner, the learned model and MPC, and the safety layer, with the weights in weight.pth.
